\chapter{Background and Related Work}

\section{Biomedical Information Retrieval}

PubMed is a free, searchable database of more than 40 million citations and abstracts for biomedical and life sciences literature. It was developed and is maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) at the U.S. National Library of Medicine (NLM), which is part of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and it remains the leading literature search tool for health professionals, researchers, and the general public.\footnote{\url{https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/about/}}

% evidence: biomed_knowledge_platform/src/biomed_platform/core/use_cases/document_fetch.py

Biomedical information retrieval also has a long benchmark tradition. BioASQ framed large-scale biomedical semantic indexing and question answering as a combined retrieval and synthesis problem over biomedical literature, with expert-created questions, retrieved snippets, and ideal answers \cite{tsatsaronis2015bioasq}. The TREC Precision Medicine Track similarly emphasized retrieval for clinically structured cases, where evidence ranking must account for disease, gene, variant, and treatment relevance rather than superficial textual overlap \cite{roberts2020trecpm}. These benchmark traditions justify evaluating retrieval as its own stage. If relevant evidence is absent from the retrieved context, no downstream generator can reliably recover the missing source.

Recent biomedical retrieval work has moved beyond purely lexical matching. MedCPT trains contrastive biomedical retrievers and rerankers from large-scale PubMed search logs, showing that user-query and click behavior can support zero-shot biomedical information retrieval \cite{jin2023medcpt}. BioBERT and related biomedical language models further demonstrate that domain-specific pretraining improves biomedical text understanding compared with general-domain language representations \cite{lee2020biobert}. Sentence-transformer architectures provide the general dense embedding basis for semantic similarity search \cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. The discussion above establishes that indexing biomedical text as embedded chunks within a vector database is a highly effective retrieval strategy. However, the literature also demonstrates that the choice of embedding model, chunking strategy, and ranking algorithm are critical empirical variables that directly dictate system performance, rather than minor implementation details. Consequently, evaluating a biomedical retrieval framework requires isolating these distinct variables to measure their individual impact on system accuracy.

\section{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) couples a parametric language model with retrieved non-parametric evidence at inference time. Lewis et al. introduced this paradigm for knowledge-intensive natural language processing tasks, arguing that retrieved passages can improve factual specificity and provide an updateable memory external to the model parameters \cite{lewis2020rag}. This distinction is central for biomedical work. Biomedical knowledge changes continuously, and relying only on a model's parametric memory would make the system dependent on opaque training data and a fixed knowledge cutoff.

In a biomedical setting, RAG functions primarily as an information-delivery system that grounds the model in existing literature, rather than an absolute assurance of clinical correctness. It minimizes errors by providing source documents, but cannot completely prevent inaccurate outputs. The retrieval stage selects candidate evidence from the indexed corpus, the synthesis stage generates an answer conditioned on the selected context, and citation handling links answer content back to retrieved sources. The architecture therefore aligns with the literature's core motivation for RAG: explicit access to external evidence and clearer provenance than closed-book generation.

Several medical RAG systems illustrate the same direction. Clinfo.ai uses scientific literature retrieval to answer medical questions and explicitly evaluates retrieval and synthesis behavior \cite{lozano2024clinfo}. Almanac applies retrieval-augmented language modeling to clinical medicine and includes physician evaluation over clinical scenarios \cite{zakka2024almanac}. Benchmarking Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Medicine introduces MIRAGE and the MedRAG toolkit to compare corpora, retrievers, and models across medical question answering datasets \cite{xiong2024benchmarking}. Across these systems, retrieval quality, generation quality, and end-task performance are treated as distinct dimensions requiring separate evaluation, rather than being inferred from generated-answer quality alone.

The literature also motivates configurable retrieval expansion. Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) generate a synthetic answer-like passage from the query and embed that generated text to retrieve semantically related documents without requiring relevance labels \cite{gao2023hyde}. This approach reframes retrieval as a generation-then-embedding problem, under the assumption that a plausible hypothetical answer shares more semantic structure with relevant documents than the original query does in isolation. Its expected benefit is improved recall for underspecified or loosely phrased queries, where lexical or dense query-side representations may fail to surface semantically relevant evidence. Its principal risk is expansion noise: when the original query is already precise, the generated passage may introduce spurious content that shifts retrieval away from the most relevant documents rather than toward them. This tradeoff positions HyDE as a configurable retrieval strategy whose benefit is query-dependent, rather than as a universally beneficial enhancement.

\section{Biomedical RAG Challenges}

Biomedical RAG inherits the difficulties of biomedical information retrieval and adds generation-specific risks. The first challenge is terminology. Biomedical questions often mix clinical language, mechanistic terminology, abbreviations, disease names, procedures, and outcome measures. A thrombosis-oriented question may refer to venous thromboembolism, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, catheter failure, anticoagulation, or postoperative risk using different textual forms. Dense retrieval can reduce exact-term dependence, but it can also retrieve semantically nearby yet clinically misaligned evidence. This makes provenance-bearing metadata and post-retrieval evaluation essential.

The second challenge is corpus currency. PubMed-based systems operate over a literature stream that changes continuously. Static corpora are easier to reproduce, but they progressively diverge from the current state of the literature; continuously updated corpora better preserve currency, but they require identifier tracking, deduplication, and run-level traceability. The literature on RAG in healthcare identifies up-to-date external evidence as one of the main motivations for RAG, while also emphasizing that healthcare RAG still lacks standardized evaluation and governance practices \cite{amugongo2025healthcarerag}.

The third challenge is grounding. A generated answer can be fluent, relevant, and still unsupported by the retrieved sources. Conversely, a response can be faithful to retrieved context that is incomplete, outdated, or not clinically authoritative. This distinction is especially important in medicine. Evaluations of medical RAG systems show improvements from retrieval in some clinical tasks, but also conclude that RAG-supported outputs remain insufficient for autonomous clinical use \cite{malik2024anticoagulationrag}. This distinction between fluency, faithfulness, and clinical authority underscores that retrieval grounding alone does not guarantee clinical validity, particularly when retrieved context is itself incomplete or outdated.

The fourth challenge is corpus granularity. Biomedical articles contain titles, abstracts, methods, results, discussion sections, and bibliographic metadata. Retrieval over entire documents can hide relevant passages; retrieval over very small fragments can lose context. The implemented system uses chunked indexing with preserved metadata so that retrieval can operate at chunk level while answers retain article-level provenance. This design follows the broader movement toward passage- and snippet-level biomedical retrieval seen in BioASQ and related systems, while keeping the implementation bounded enough for MSc-level evaluation.

\section{Evaluation of RAG Systems}

Evaluating RAG systems requires isolating distinct failure modes, because retrieval errors, grounding errors, and task-output errors arise from fundamentally different algorithmic bottlenecks. Retrieval metrics such as normalized discounted cumulative gain, precision at k, recall at k, and mean reciprocal rank measure whether relevant sources are ranked highly enough to enter the answer context. Normalized discounted cumulative gain is particularly appropriate when relevance is graded because it rewards ranking more useful evidence earlier \cite{jarvelin2002ndcg}. Benchmarking information retrieval (BEIR), the standard evaluation suite for information retrieval models, reinforces the need to test retrieval systems across heterogeneous tasks rather than relying on a single narrow benchmark \cite{thakur2021beir}. In the biomedical domain, BioASQ and PubMedQA further show that biomedical question answering requires evidence selection and answer evaluation over biomedical abstracts and expert-derived labels \cite{tsatsaronis2015bioasq,jin2019pubmedqa}.

Answer-level evaluation adds a separate layer. RAGAS proposes reference-free metrics for RAG pipelines, including faithfulness, answer relevancy, and context precision \cite{es2024ragas}. These metrics match the evaluation subsystem in this repository: answer relevancy assesses whether the response addresses the question, faithfulness estimates whether answer claims are supported by retrieved contexts, and context precision estimates whether retrieved contexts are useful for the answer. However, these metrics should not be interpreted as biomedical truth verification. They diagnose grounding behavior relative to supplied context.
% evidence: evaluation_metrics/src/phases/phase4_ragas.py

Other RAG evaluation frameworks support this decomposition. ARES evaluates context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance using synthetic data and smaller human-labeled validation sets \cite{saadfalcon2024ares}. RAGChecker argues for fine-grained diagnosis of retrieval and generation modules instead of a single aggregate RAG score \cite{ru2024ragchecker}. These frameworks support the phase-based evaluation design used in this thesis, where answer generation is materialized before scoring and where retrieval, RAGAS, audit, and extraction phases can be inspected separately.

For thrombosis-oriented questions, task-specific extraction metrics are also needed. A response may be grounded and relevant but still miss a risk factor or add one unsupported by the reference set. The evaluation subsystem therefore includes risk-factor extraction analysis with precision, recall, and F1 over a closed canonical vocabulary. This aligns with biomedical NLP practice, where extraction tasks are usually evaluated against curated labels rather than only against free-form textual impressions.

\section{Human Validation Workflows}

Human validation is a methodological requirement in biomedical NLP because gold-standard labels are rarely self-evident. Deleger et al. describe gold-standard corpus construction for medical NLP using double annotation, consensus, and inter-annotator agreement measurement \cite{deleger2012goldcorpora}. To achieve this, the task utilized a double-annotation workflow where two independent medical experts manually reviewed each medical record. Any conflicting assessments between the reviewers were then resolved through a formal human adjudication process. This collaborative workflow successfully established a highly reliable, ground-truth dataset that can serve as a trusted baseline for evaluating automated systems, demonstrating how rigorous expert annotation schemas support reproducible evaluation in clinical information extraction \cite{stubbs2015i2b2risk}.

Human-in-the-loop workflows can also improve annotation efficiency without removing human responsibility. Neveol et al. found that semi-automatic semantic annotation of PubMed queries reduced manual effort while preserving or improving annotation quality \cite{neveol2011semiannotation}. This motivates a pragmatic thesis workflow: software can prepare candidate tasks, normalize metadata, and structure exports, while human review remains responsible for judging label meaning and evidence adequacy.

The repository uses a human curation workflow, with supporting tooling that transforms biomedical abstract collections into review tasks and exports reviewed annotations for downstream processing. Its value here is operational rather than epistemic. It provides a traceable interface for human review, while repository tooling preserves DOI, PMID, title, abstract, and annotation fields for evaluation. The current thesis therefore treats curated labels as a reproducible reference substrate, not as a fully adjudicated clinical gold standard.

This boundary is important. Human validation in the thesis supports literature curation and evaluation of risk-factor extraction behavior. It does not establish prospective clinical safety, clinician workflow benefit, or treatment validity. Those claims would require a different study design, domain-expert adjudication protocol, and clinical evaluation setting.

\section{Local LLM Deployment Considerations}

The local deployment of the LLM is relevant to biomedical RAG for privacy, reproducibility, and operational control. Healthcare studies have explored local or privacy-preserving LLM pipelines for clinical text review and information retrieval. Vaid et al. used a local open-source LLM with retrieval over historic echocardiogram reports, showing that local deployment can support clinician-facing retrieval tasks while still producing errors that require validation \cite{vaid2024local}. Wiest et al. similarly evaluated privacy-preserving LLM use for structured medical information retrieval, demonstrating the feasibility of local clinical text extraction while comparing model outputs against expert-derived references \cite{wiest2024privacyllm}.

Local deployment does not by itself make a system safe. Reviews of LLMs in medicine emphasize hallucination, bias, validation, and governance risks \cite{thirunavukarasu2023llmmedicine}. Open medical LLM deployment also requires transparent model provenance, version control, and auditable update processes \cite{riedemann2024openmedicine}.
% evidence: biomed_knowledge_platform/configs/llm.yaml
% evidence: llm_server/docker-compose.yml

Local LLM deployment cannot be established from the model name alone. A reproducible run should record the model family, model tag or digest, quantization, context length, temperature, seed where supported, prompt template, embedding model, vector index state, and corpus snapshot. The Llama 3 model report describes the model family and release context \cite{dubey2024llama3}; however, a local quantized runtime is still a specific deployment artifact. In addition, local execution reduces third-party data transfer but does not eliminate privacy risk: language models can memorize and emit training data under some conditions \cite{carlini2021extracting}.

\section{Research Gaps and Thesis Positioning}

The reviewed literature establishes mature foundations for biomedical retrieval, RAG, biomedical QA evaluation, annotation workflows, and local LLM deployment. The gap addressed by this thesis is not the invention of a new biomedical language model or a new clinical benchmark. Instead, the thesis targets the integration problem: how to combine continuous PubMed-oriented acquisition, chunked vector indexing, evidence-grounded synthesis, human curation, and reproducible evaluation in one repository-grounded research artifact.

Existing biomedical IR benchmarks motivate rigorous retrieval evaluation, but they do not by themselves define an operational ingestion architecture. Medical RAG systems demonstrate the value of retrieved evidence, but they often differ in corpora, retrievers, models, and evaluation protocols, making direct comparison difficult. RAG evaluation frameworks provide useful grounding diagnostics, but they do not verify biomedical correctness. Human validation workflows create more credible reference data, but they require explicit schema design and annotation governance. Local LLM deployment improves control, but it introduces model-version and resource constraints.

This thesis is positioned at the intersection of these gaps. Its contribution is an evidence-aware biomedical RAG platform and evaluation workflow for medical literature questions, demonstrated through the use case of identifying literature that reports risk factors related to thrombosis. The platform is intentionally modular: the biomedical RAG service owns ingestion, retrieval, and synthesis; the scheduler service owns continuous literature acquisition; the evaluation subsystem owns phase-based measurement; and the human curation workflow supports curated reference artifacts. This positioning keeps the thesis claims defensible. The work evaluates a concrete system configuration under documented constraints, rather than claiming general clinical validity or universal superiority over other biomedical RAG architectures.
